Modelling the growth of razor clam (Siliqua patula) in Washington State
coast



Summary

      We propose a method for fitting growth curves to capture and recapture
data of razor clam (RC)(Siliqua patula) when the age  at  first  capture  is
unknown. 75 and 97 RCs were capture and recapture from the Copalis and  Long
Beach in Washington State coast  between  year  2005  to  2007.   The  shell
length and width of RCs were measured during  capture  and  recapture(s).  A
comparison of linear and nonlinear relationship  between  shell  length  and
width of  RCs  with  Akaike  information  Criteria  is  performed.  The  von
Bertalanffy growth curve is  used  to  model  the  growth.  To  account  for
individual variability, the unknown age in square transformation of a RC  at
first recapture, and the individual growth coefficient of its  shell  length
or  width  are  modeled  as  random  effects   with   a   bivariate   normal
distribution.  A  comparison  between  this  model  and  the  Fabens  growth
equation is given. The proposed new model results  with  better  statistical
properties compared  with  Fabens  model.  It  provides  consistent,  robust
results for both shell length and width of capture  and  recapture(s)  data.
Moreover, the fitting of  model  is  amenable  to  existing  software.   The
proposed method is a flexible method and can be  applied  to  fit  different
growth equations when the age at first capture is unknown.



Introduction

      The tens of thousands of visitors who are drawn to Washington State's
coastal communities to recreationally harvest razor clams continue to leave
a significant economic contribution in their wake. This economic
contribution is especially important during the quiet fall, winter and
early spring months. As a result of input received at annual razor clam
public meetings, WDFW has attempted over the last several years to allow
more harvest during the October through March period than in previous
years. This change in season setting was an attempt to specifically improve
the economic impact of this fishery and has been very successful. A recent
example was a turnout of 21,000 diggers on the evening of November 23,
2007, (part of a 4-day post-Thanksgiving holiday opener.

      The razor clam fishery in Washington is not only a significant source
of revenue for tourism-dependent businesses such as restaurants and motels,
but also an important source of community identity and basis for tribal
subsistence. Razor clam harvesting, cleaning, cooking, eating, and canning
have been an important focus of family relationships and local culture in
Washington coastal communities for many generations.

Methods
Beginning in February 2006, clams were planted along a total of four
transects. Two transects were located in the Copalis Razor Clam Reserve,
and two others in the Long Beach razor clam reserve. Clam were planted on
each side of each transect (north and south). Each side of each transect
was re-sampled every six-months (as weather and surf conditions allowed).
This sampling was staggered so that one side was sampled and then three
months later the other side was sampled.

A series of permanent steel posts were placed in a line perpendicular to
the surf. These were used to re-locate the exact position of each transect.


Sampling was conducted using the WDFW standard razor clam sampling
technique, the "pumped area method."  This involves using water pumped from
the surf to liquidly the sand allowing the clams that are present to float
to the surface and be sampled. At 50-foot intervals or elevations, starting
at the top of the clam beds to the edge of the surf and perpendicular along
each transect clams were sampled in six plots (each ½ square/meter in
area).  All razor clams were removed as they came to the surface. Each clam
was measured for both length and width.  Also recorded was the exact
location the clam was taken from. This included transect, side (north or
south), elevation (50, 100, 150, etc) and plot (1,2,3,4,5,6). The date was
also recorded. The clams were then tagged. Of all clams over approximately
75 mm in length, 1/3 (every third clam encountered) were marked by
attaching a PIT (passive integrate transponder) tag to the exterior of the
shell. Of all clams over approximately 75 mm in length, approximately 2/3's
were marked by etching a unique identifier on the shell. Of all clams under
approximately 75 mm in length, 100% were marked by attaching a PIT tag to
the exterior of the shell. If possible, each PIT tagged clams were also
etched with a light 'T' to assess rate of tag loss.

The PIT tag was attached using cyanoacrylate (CA) glue and an accelerator
to speed the drying time. Etching will be accomplished using a battery
operated Dremel ® drill with an etching disk attached.

Each plot was then "hardened' by adding sand to the liquefied area using a
standard garden shovel. The tagged clams were then replanted in the exact
plot from which they were taken. Care was taken to position each clam
correctly to assure their survival. Additional sand was then added over the
top of each plot.

Discussion

      We propose an alternative  model  to  Fabens  model  to  estimate  the
expected asymptotic size and expected growth  coefficient  of  RC  when  the
initial first capture age is unknown. The  proposed  model  is  parsimonious
and allows for unknown initial age and individual variability in the  growth
curves. This is an advantage as knowledge of variation in individual  growth
is very important in  developing  realistic  models  of  growth  in  RC  for
harvesting  strategies  in  fisheries.  This  model  can  apply   to   other
crustaceans, eg. abalone, red  sea  urchin,  lobster,...,etc,  where  direct
aging is impossible.
      Palmer  et  al.  (1991)  used  simulated  data  of  a   mark-recapture
experiment on rock (spiny) lobster (Panulirus cygnus) in Mexico to show  the
effectiveness of mixed effects model in estimating the  population's  growth
curve and variability in individual growth. They  considered  growth  curves
with random animal specific coefficients.  The  unknown  initial  age  of  a
captured animal was  also  regarded  as  random  and  its  distribution  was
approximated by a mixture of uniform distributions over 20  time  intervals.
Independence between the coefficients and the initial age of an  animal  was
assumed, which might be unreasonable  as  animals  with  faster  growth  are
likely to be caught earlier. Estimation is by means of the  expectation  and
maximization (EM) algorithm (Dempster et al., 1977) but  at  present,  there
is no software available for fitting the model. Instead of finite  mixtures,
Cheng and Kuk (2002, 2003) assume the distribution of  the  unknown  initial
age to be log normal. Specifically, the unknown age at  first  capture,  the
asymptotic size and the growth coefficient of carapace  length  are  modeled
as  random  effects  (Laird  and  Wares,  1982)  with  a  trivariate  normal
distribution. The error structure within all  the  consecutive  captures  of
each lobster are assumed to be  first  order  autoregressive  process  AR(1)
with unequally spaced time points. Most of lobsters were female  with  twice
or more multiple recapture  observations  that  allowed  the  estimation  of
trivariate  random  effects  and  the  autoregressive  errors  within   each
lobster. Our RC data were  limited  to  estimate  bivariate  random  effects
because most of data were limited to one  single  recapture.   We  chose  to
model ([pic]) bivariate random  effects  instead  of   ([pic])  because  the
estimated coefficient of variation (CV) of [pic] was smaller than the CV  of
[pic]. In addition, we wanted to investigate the  growth  change  or  growth
rate and the unknown age of first capture for individual RC.


      In addition to the von  Bertalanffy  curve,  there  are  other  common
growth curves such as logistic and Gompertz  (Moreau,  1987).  These  curves
can also be used with the method discussed in this paper so as to deal  with
the unknown age problem, whilst they cannot be with Fabens method.
